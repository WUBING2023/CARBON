## External binding preferences reveal learned sequence-order rules

We asked whether CARBON's sequence judgments agree with binding preferences measured outside the eCLIP training setting. After the initial HNRNPK, PTBP1 and RBFOX2 exploration, an availability-defined intersection of original RNAcompete measurements and existing model checkpoints yielded 25 additional human RBPs. We replaced the central five bases of 16 fixed native sequence contexts with every possible five-mer, held the conservation background fixed within each context, and compared the mean predicted count response with independent in-vitro preference scores. Each RBP received equal weight after averaging its available assay columns and cell backgrounds. Across these 25 RBPs, Full and Sequence achieved mean Spearman correlations of 0.257 and 0.269, respectively (panel a). Both exceeded native word-frequency ranking: Full-minus-frequency was 0.146 (95% paired protein-bootstrap interval, 0.038–0.248; 18 of 25 RBPs positive), and Sequence-minus-frequency was 0.158 (0.073–0.237; 20 of 25 positive). The Full-minus-Sequence difference was −0.012 (−0.067–0.039). Existing Full and Sequence checkpoints are not established as matched training interventions; these results evaluate model-family rule correctness rather than identify a causal conservation effect.

To distinguish sequence arrangement from base composition, we next compared only pairs of five-mers containing exactly the same numbers of A, C, G and U bases. Prediction ties received half credit and experimental ties were excluded. Full and Sequence correctly ordered 56.62% and 57.31% of comparable pairs, with each model above the 50% reference in 23 of 25 RBPs (panel b). Their differences from frequency-based ordering were 3.78 percentage points (0.90–6.73; 19 of 25 positive) and 4.47 points (1.28–7.51; 20 of 25 positive), respectively. Base-count and fixed-input conservation-only rules cannot distinguish words within these composition groups. Frozen Full models given flat or permuted conservation achieved 56.23% and 56.36%; these input perturbations test dependence on the supplied background and do not substitute for retrained ablations. Thus, the family learned externally supported ordering rules beyond simply counting favored bases, while Full did not show a general ordering advantage over Sequence.

We also examined whether a local attribution-derived priority list can recover externally preferred words. This analysis retained all 20 highest-scoring HNRNPK RNAcompete words and all six matched fits across two cell backgrounds and three training seeds (panel c). Native-sequence integrated-gradient priority recovered 29.17% of the external top 20 for Full, versus 21.67% for Sequence, 25.83% for trained Mean and 24.17% for trained Perm. The Full-minus-Sequence difference was 7.50 percentage points, with a conditional 95% interval of 1.67–9.17 from 2,000 paired resamples of 385 gene/locus clusters (panel d). Corresponding Full-minus-Mean and Full-minus-Perm intervals crossed zero. The local improvement extended to C-rich words containing three or fewer cytosines; it did not arise solely from four-or-more-C words, for which Full and Sequence had identical contributions to recall. A simple C-count rule achieved 25% under lexicographic tie breaking but 12.89% under unbiased boundary-tie averaging, compared with 10% for native word frequency. These baseline sensitivities were retained rather than used to redefine the primary ranking endpoint.

The HNRNPK result was assay specific: against the independently measured RBNS preferences at the preselected 20 nM column, Full-minus-Sequence recall was −4.17 points (conditional interval, −5.00–0.83). RNAcompete and RBNS differ in RNA pools and measurement scales, and the current evidence does not identify the cause of this disagreement. The local example therefore supports improved prioritization relative to Sequence for one external assay, not a generally correct functional mechanism or a conservation-specific advantage. Separate fixed component diagnostics further separated sequence-word preference from native-site allocation. For PTBP1, Full had lower whole-word correlation than NoGRU (0.653 versus 0.685), but higher same-composition ordering (59.76% versus 57.42%), mainly in K562; Sequence remained higher at 62.16%. For HNRNPK RNAcompete, Full was lower than NoGRU for both whole-word correlation (0.648 versus 0.669) and same-composition ordering (61.93% versus 62.33%); its whole-word advantage for the fixed RBNS column did not extend to above-reference same-composition ordering. All assay columns, cell backgrounds, seeds, necessary controls and negative outcomes were retained. The two external analyses support experimentally anchored sequence rules and a bounded local prioritization improvement, while leaving a general conservation explanation advantage unestablished.
